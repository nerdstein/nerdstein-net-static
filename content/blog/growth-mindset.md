> "In a fixed mindset, students believe their basic abilities, their intelligence, their talents, are just fixed traits. They have a certain amount and that's that. In a growth mindset, students understand that their talents and abilities can be developed through effort, good teaching and persistence."
> — Carol Dweck, *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success*

Growth mindset has become one of those terms that gets deployed so often it's started to lose texture. You see it in company values decks, in performance review templates, in motivational posters in office lobbies. Which is a shame, because the original idea — Carol Dweck's research on fixed vs. growth orientations toward ability — is genuinely useful if you engage with it carefully rather than as a slogan.

The version I find most practical isn't about believing you can get better at anything. It's narrower than that: it's about how you relate to failure and difficulty in a specific domain. A growth mindset in engineering means treating a hard bug not as evidence that you're not smart enough, but as a puzzle with a solution that you haven't found yet. It means asking for feedback without treating it as a referendum on your worth. It means the discomfort of not knowing something is a signal to learn, not a reason to avoid the territory.

What makes this hard in practice is that most organizational systems reward outcomes, not learning. You're measured on whether the thing shipped, not on how much you grew in the process of shipping it. So people develop a split consciousness — growth mindset in the language they use, fixed mindset in the incentives they actually respond to. Closing that gap requires leaders to actively protect learning-oriented behavior even when it's slower and messier than the alternative.

{{< callout >}}
If you want to build a team with a genuine growth orientation, the most important thing you can do is model what it looks like to not know something and be okay with it. Say "I don't know, let's figure it out" in front of the people you lead. Make the behavior visible, not just aspirational.
{{< /callout >}}

The other piece Dweck's research points to is effort. Fixed mindset treats high effort as a sign that you're not talented — talented people don't have to try as hard. Growth mindset inverts this: effort is how you get better. That's a useful reframe for engineering teams specifically, where there's often an ambient belief that the best engineers make everything look effortless. They don't. They've just done the effortful part in private, or over a long time, and what you're seeing is the output of accumulated investment.
