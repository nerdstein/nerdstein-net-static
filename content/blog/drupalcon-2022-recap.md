The last time I walked the hallways of a DrupalCon, I didn't know it would be the last time for two years. Portland 2022 wasn't just a conference — it was a collective exhale. You could feel it in the way people stood around talking after sessions ended, in no particular hurry to be anywhere else. The energy of a community that had been conducting itself entirely through Zoom calls, relearning what it actually feels like to be in the same room.

The sessions I got the most out of weren't the big keynotes. They rarely are. It was the smaller rooms where practitioners were working through specific, thorny problems — CI/CD pipelines for Drupal 9, decoupled architecture patterns, the state of automated testing in the contrib ecosystem. These are the kinds of conversations that don't translate well to blog posts or YouTube replays, because the value lives in the Q&A and the hallway threads that spin off afterward. A speaker says something that's 80% right, and then someone in the third row makes it 100% right by asking the question that everyone else was half-thinking. That's the hallway track, formalized.

I came away from Portland thinking about community sustainability more than I have in years. Drupal is at an interesting inflection point — not in crisis, but facing real questions about how to attract the next generation of contributors while retaining the institutional knowledge of people who've been building on it for a decade. The answer isn't more documentation or better onboarding flows, though those help. It's fundamentally about whether the community feels like a place worth belonging to. I think it does. But "I think so" isn't a strategy, and the conversations I had in Portland convinced me that more people need to be asking the harder version of that question out loud.
