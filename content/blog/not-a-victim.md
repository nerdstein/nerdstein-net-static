There's a particular kind of paralysis that comes from locating all of your problems in external causes. The organization is dysfunctional. The community has bad incentives. The leadership doesn't get it. The system is rigged. These things are often true — and also, they're not especially useful as an endpoint. What you do with the truth about a difficult situation matters more than how accurately you've diagnosed it.

I've been thinking about this in the context of open source communities, where the grievance cycle can become almost self-sustaining. Someone does something harmful. People respond with frustration, which sometimes turns into permanent posture — a kind of practiced outrage that becomes load-bearing to someone's identity within the community. I understand the impulse. Some situations are genuinely bad and deserve sustained attention. But there's a difference between holding people accountable and organizing your participation around your wounds.

The reframe I keep coming back to is agency. Not the naive version — not "just decide to be happy" or "ignore the bad things." The more practical version: given the actual constraints of this situation, what can I change? What can I influence? What is genuinely outside my control, and what have I been treating as outside my control because changing it would be hard? The last category is usually larger than I want it to be.

Owning your narrative doesn't mean pretending everything is fine. It means refusing to hand the authorship of your story over to circumstances or to other people's behavior. The situation can be genuinely bad and you can still decide what you do next. In fact, that's the only moment you ever get to make that decision — now, in the situation as it actually is, not the situation you wish you were in instead.
